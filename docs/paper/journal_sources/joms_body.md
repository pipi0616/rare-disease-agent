## Introduction

A patient with a rare disease often waits years for a diagnosis. Phenotype-driven tools were built to shorten that wait: given the patient's findings as Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms [1], they rank the diseases whose annotated phenotype fits best [2–5]. Large language models (LLMs) have since been put to the same question, asked directly [6–9] and as agents or pipelines that call such tools and search the literature [10–13]. The reported accuracies are high — a recent agentic system ranks the confirmed diagnosis first in 57% of cases on average across nine datasets [10] — and hard to read, for three reasons.

**Benchmarks leak through the knowledge base.** The public test cases are published case reports, and HPO's disease annotations are curated from published case reports — often the same ones. A tool that reads the annotations while it is tested on the patient they were written from is, in part, reading the answer. Standard corpora and frameworks for benchmarking these tools exist [14,15], but the overlap is rarely controlled. One recent preprint removed it and found a published tool's first-place accuracy on Phenopacket Store cases fall from 48.7% to 17.2% [13], and an audit of a reconstructed agent found the same kind of leak through its bank of similar cases [16].

**A model may have read the test.** A benchmark public before a model's training data ends can be memorised, and a model asked directly, and any agent built on it, then looks stronger than it is.

**Published cases are not patients.** A case report describes a patient the authors chose, after the diagnosis was known. Patients as clinics and laboratories record them have fewer findings, and more of them have the commoner rare diseases [17,18].

We built a deliberately simple agent — one inexpensive model, five or six tools over pinned public knowledge, about one US cent per case — and compared it throughout with the same model asked directly, so that what it adds can be traced to its tools rather than to scale. We mask each case's source paper from everything the agent's tools read, the bank of similar patients included, and measure how much a benchmark of published cases leaks; show that a held-out test from the development source missed a failure that external patients exposed; and test two revisions of the agent, under one plan, on three sets whose cases were never used in their development — patients shared by families and clinicians (MyGene2 [19,20]), patients of diagnostic laboratories and clinics (CADA [21]), and case reports that entered PubMed after the model's training cutoff. Every prespecified test is reported against its plan, committed beforehand, including the three prespecified claims that failed.

## Methods

### Task and scoring

A system is given a patient's findings as HPO terms, and the patient's sex where the data set records it (Phenopacket Store), and nothing else; findings recorded as absent are withheld from every system. It answers with up to ten diseases, best first, each with an OMIM or Orphanet identifier. A case is solved at rank *k* when one of the first *k* identifiers is the confirmed diagnosis, that is, any code the source lists for the patient (Online Resource 1, S1); the tests run in November 2026 also accept an identifier that Mondo [22] (release 2026-09-01) matches exactly, while the October runs (v1.0's tests, v1.1a and their comparators) were scored by the identifiers as written. For a disease with genetic subtypes this asks for the subtype. A case without an answer is a miss. Rates carry 95% Wilson intervals [23]. Two systems are compared on the same cases by the two-sided exact sign test on the cases only one of them solves ("a : b"); paired differences with Wald intervals were added after the tests; the three primary tests of the revised agent are corrected together by Holm's method [24], and other p-values are unadjusted. Answers were also judged by name with DeepRare's prompt [10], which shows a model the first five names and the confirmed diagnosis and asks at which rank, if any, it is named; the judge was `deepseek-flash`, the model inside the systems it judged. Prespecified claims are decided by identifiers. The study is reported following TRIPOD+AI [31] (Online Resource 2).

### Cases

[[T:sets]] lists every set, how it was used and which version it tested; Online Resource 1, sections S1 and S3, gives details.

**Development and held-out cases.** Phenopacket Store 0.1.27 [14,25] holds 10,374 published patients with a confirmed OMIM diagnosis from 1,733 papers. Papers were split before any development into a design split (6,014 patients, also the bank of similar patients one tool searches) and 840 held-out papers, one case each; a fixed random 200 of these were used for other providers' models and DeepRare.

**Temporal and external tests of the first version.** The temporal test takes one case from each paper first public on or after 1 June 2025, the reported end of the model's training data, and not used in development: 21 held-out papers and 26 added to Phenopacket Store after release 0.1.27. RareBench's [6] HMS (88 patients of one clinic) and RAMEDIS (624 patients of a metabolic registry) were external tests and then, once their errors had been read, development sets ([[T:sets]]).

**Prespecified tests of the revised agent**, fixed in one plan before any system ran on them; no one had read their cases:

- *MyGene2* [20]: features and causal genes shared by families and clinicians, as preprocessed for SHEPHERD [19] (146 patients with an OMIM diagnosis). Distal arthrogryposis types account for 54 patients. A different 54 patients name a source paper; it was masked.
- *CADA test set* [21]: of the authors' test file, the 577 patients with an OMIM diagnosis — 239 ClinVar submissions, whose label is the condition a laboratory asserted for a pathogenic variant; 205 patients of seven cohorts of the CADA authors' collaborators (Tübingen and others); and 133 of the PEDIA cohort, of whom 108 were submitted by named individuals and 25 are labelled as published cases. No CADA case names its source, so none could be masked. Median five findings. Because 300 cases of the authors' training file were a development set, the test file is held out from a development source rather than external.
- *Case reports after the cutoff* (Online Resource 1, S3): PubMed case reports with open full text that entered PubMed between 1 June 2025 and 24 October 2026 and were first public on or after 1 June 2025. A report was kept when its title named one disease that Orphanet lists [27] and two automatic annotators — PubTator3 [28] and the model — agreed on it. The case description from the PMC BioC service [29] was cut, without rewriting, before the first sentence that names the disease or its gene, mentions a confirming test, or was pointed to by the model, and read into HPO terms by the note reader (described below). Diseases were drawn to a test or a development side; the test side holds 240 reports of 142 diseases (median ten findings); 141 have a diagnosis with no OMIM entry, and many are not Mendelian. An audit of 30 development-side reports found the key right in 29 and the diagnosis still readable in 3 (95% CI 3.5–25.6%).

### Knowledge and masking

The tools read HPO's disease annotations (release 2026-09-01; 8,478 OMIM diseases) and, in v2, Orphanet's as well, merged by Mondo's exact matches. From v1.1 a sixth tool searches disease descriptions from Orphanet and MedlinePlus Genetics, and each candidate shown to the model carries its Orphanet prevalence class. While a system works on a case, the case's source paper is masked from all of it ([[F:design]]): annotations supported only by that paper are removed; a disease known only from it does not exist for any tool; and its patients leave the bank of similar patients. The mask removes whole papers, including what they say about their other patients, but cannot remove what cites no paper: 30.6% of HPO's phenotype annotations of OMIM diseases cite only OMIM entries, and Orphanet's annotations, the descriptions and the prevalence classes cite none. It is therefore neither an upper nor a lower bound on the leak. After each run every tool output is searched for the case's paper and for diseases known only from it; a single hit voids the run.

### The agent

All versions run on LangChain's agent loop with `deepseek-flash`, reasoning off, temperature 0, and at most eight model calls in the loop, plus, from v1.1, one or two for the model's first answer. The tools rank every disease by phenotype similarity (Resnik, averaged over both directions [2]), check one disease against the findings, look a disease up, find an identifier from a name, find the closest published patients, and (from v1.1) search disease descriptions. **v1.0** starts from the tools' ten best candidates ("rank, then check": phenotype similarity's top thirty reordered by how many of the patient's findings each explains), each with its match report. **v1.1** first answers from the model's own knowledge; each disease it names is looked up and checked like the tools' candidates, with each candidate's Orphanet prevalence class shown, and the model is asked to prefer the more common of comparably fitting candidates ("relatively common": one patient in 100,000 or more). **v2** adds Orphanet's diseases to the knowledge, takes the tools' list from a one-way similarity (the patient's findings looked for in the disease [2,5]), and a fixed rule then puts the model's own first answer first when Orphanet classes it relatively common and the one-way ranking has it in its top thirty. An earlier form of v1.1 (v1.1a) was run without a prespecified comparison (Online Resource 1, Table S11). Prompts are in Online Resource 1, S4.

Answers shorter than ten are filled as described in Online Resource 1, S4. A rule chose v1.1 for MyGene2 and CADA, whose diagnoses carry OMIM identifiers, because v2 was not better on the four of five planned development sets that were run (Online Resource 1, S2); the plan states that the rule was written before those results were seen, but it was first committed together with them. Testing v2 on the case reports, many of whose diagnoses v1.1's knowledge does not contain, was decided afterwards. Clinical notes are read into HPO terms by a separate note reader (`deepseek-flash`), which quotes each finding with its status and never writes an identifier (Online Resource 1, S5); its prespecified primary claim — better than both baselines on clinical notes — failed: it beat the model writing identifiers (58 : 5) but not a dictionary of HPO names (29 : 38, p = 0.33).

### Comparators

The same model asked once without tools ("asked directly"; asked for an OMIM identifier on the held-out, temporal and external sets, and for an OMIM or Orphanet identifier on the three later tests); a fixed pipeline (the tools' top ten ranked by one model call); three rankings without a model — phenotype similarity, the nearest published patients, and "rank, then check" (v1.0's starting list); LIRICAL 2.4.1 [3], phenotype only, on OMIM's diseases, with each case's own masked annotation file; GPT-6.1, Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Gemini 4 asked directly with the same prompt, at their lowest reasoning setting; and DeepRare [10], run from its published code on the agent's model with its case bank limited to the public RareArena cases [26] and the other substitutions in Online Resource 1, Table S8. DeepRare searches the web and could not be masked; its reading was searched for each patient's paper. It answers with names, matched to OMIM entries to score it by identifier; as this understates it (Online Resource 1, S8), it is also judged by name. Model identifiers and dates are in Online Resource 1, S12.

### Plans and records

Each prespecified test followed a plan committed to the project's git repository before its first run, fixing the cases, the systems and their version, the comparison that decides the primary claim, and that each system is scored once. The plans state twelve primary claims; nine held and three failed (Online Resource 1, S7), and primary claims were not adjusted across plans. One plan was changed, with a dated note, after part of its results had been seen: questions lost to API failures were asked again instead of the run being voided. The plans are written in Chinese and timed by the authors' git client; Online Resource 1, S7, lists each plan's commit. Analyses not named in a plan are marked as unplanned.

## Results

### Masking halves a published tool's first-place accuracy

LIRICAL ranked the confirmed disease first in 63.2% of the 840 held-out cases unmasked and in 30.1% masked (278 cases lost, none gained). For three quarters of the cases HPO's annotation of the confirmed disease cites the case's own paper, and the whole difference comes from them. Part of it is that 122 cases cannot be answered under the mask at all — for 119 the disease is known to HPO only from the patient's own paper, for 3 HPO lacks it; on the 718 cases that stay answerable, LIRICAL still falls from 62.4% to 35.2%. The project's own rankings lose as much ([[F:mask]]).

### The first version led on masked held-out cases

On the 840 held-out cases v1.0 ranked the confirmed disease first in 42.4% (95% CI 39.1–45.7) ([[F:heldout]]a; [[T:heldout]]). Its prespecified claims held: against the fixed pipeline 57 : 10 (p = 4 × 10⁻⁹) and against LIRICAL 121 : 18; against its own model asked directly (14.3%) it was 257 : 21. On the fixed 200 ([[F:heldout]]b) it was ahead of GPT-6.1 (37 : 17, p = 0.009), Gemini 4 (46 : 14) and Claude Sonnet 5.5 (52 : 13) asked directly, and of DeepRare by identifier (71 : 2) and by name (48 : 18); DeepRare ran under conditions not its own (see Comparators), and the result does not bear on its published figures.

### The first version lost to its own model on external patients

Two plans tested v1.0 once more ([[F:agentmodel]]a). On the 47 temporal cases it solved 23.4% and its model 8.5% (9 : 2, p = 0.065): not established. On two external patient sets it lost to its own model: 19.3% against 31.8% on HMS (4 : 15, p = 0.019) and 24.7% against 32.7% on RAMEDIS (47 : 97, p < 0.001); the prespecified claim against the fixed pipeline, on the two sets pooled, also failed (54 : 45, p = 0.42). Judged by name, DeepRare was ahead of v1.0 on HMS (38.6% against 20.5%; 8 : 24, p = 0.007).

The traces pointed to ranking, not to missing candidates. Where the model alone was right and v1.0 wrong, v1.0's own top ten held the answer in 12 of 15 HMS and 85 of 97 RAMEDIS cases, and v1.0 had usually checked it before preferring a rarer disease with a richer annotation. The tools score fit, not prevalence, and OMIM describes the commoner rare diseases thinly — Behçet disease has 15 annotated findings in OMIM and 85 in Orphanet. The held-out set had hidden this: only 42 of its 840 cases have a relatively common disease. On those 42 the model asked directly solved 61.9% and v1.0 57.1%; on the 272 rarer than one in a million, 9.6% against 52.6% (unplanned). v1.1 was built to use what the model knew. On RAMEDIS, by then a development set, v1.1a was still behind the model asked directly (30.6% against 32.7%; 56 : 69).

### Two revisions led their own model in all three prespecified tests

On **MyGene2**, v1.1 solved 52.1% (95% CI 44.0–60.0) and its model asked directly 7.5% (4.3–13.0): 69 : 4, a difference of 44.5 points (35.6–53.4). On the **CADA test set**, 23.6% (20.3–27.2) against 14.7% (12.1–17.9): 73 : 22, 8.8 points (5.6–12.1). On the **case reports**, v2 solved 19.6% (15.1–25.1) against 8.8% (5.8–13.0): 30 : 4, 10.8 points (6.3–15.4). Holm-adjusted, p < 0.001 for each ([[F:agentmodel]]b; [[F:newtests]]; [[T:tests]], which also gives the top three, five and ten). The agent was also ahead of its tools without a model and of LIRICAL on every set ([[T:tests]]; on the case reports LIRICAL reads only OMIM's diseases, which include 99 of the 240 diagnoses).

### What the lead is made of

**Counting by disease, and by name.** Counted by disease (unplanned), the agent solved more of a disease's patients than its model for 14 diseases and fewer for 2 on MyGene2, 53 against 13 on CADA and 28 against 4 on the case reports. Judged by name, the model gains (MyGene2 13.7%, CADA 18.7%) and the agent's lead remains on MyGene2 (66 : 7) and on CADA as a whole (75 : 40, p = 0.001). Looking the model's answers up the way the agent looks up its own leaves it unchanged on both sets (unplanned): its errors are rarely invalid codes, and where the name judge credits it, it named the right disease with a wrong identifier, on CADA mostly the wrong subtype.

**Where it is weaker** (descriptive). On CADA the lead was clear for the ClinVar submissions (18 : 4) and for the PEDIA cohort (36 : 8), but not for the 205 patients of the collaborators' seven cohorts (20.0% against 15.6%; 19 : 10, p = 0.14); judged by name (unplanned) it was not significant for the ClinVar submissions or the collaborators' cohorts ([[T:cada]]). Without the 25 cases labelled as published (unplanned), the agent solved 22.8% of the remaining 552 and its model 14.9% (65 : 21, p < 0.001; judged by name 69 : 34, p < 0.001). By number of findings the lead on CADA was small with up to five findings and clear from six on ([[F:findings]]; Online Resource 1, Table S10), a pattern partly confounded with cohort — 111 of the 260 cases with six or more findings are from the PEDIA cohort.

**On the case reports, roughly half to two-thirds of the lead is retrieving the correct entry** ([[T:idkind]]; unplanned). Judged by name, the agent solved 21.7% and its model 18.3% (18 : 10, p = 0.18). On the 141 reports without an OMIM entry the model named the right disease first 24 times but gave its identifier 3 times — the Orphanet codes it wrote mostly exist but name other diseases — and the agent gave it 20 times. On the 99 reports with an OMIM disease the two scorings point the same way (12 : 3 by identifier, p = 0.035; 11 : 4 by name, p = 0.12). Of the 10.8-point lead, 5.0 points remain against the model's answers looked up by name (21 : 9) and 3.3 under the name judge (18 : 10), so 54–69% of it is retrieval.

### Comparison with a frontier model, and cost

GPT-6.1 asked directly on MyGene2 solved 54.8% (46.7–62.6), not significantly different from v1.1 (22 : 26; −2.7 points, −12.0 to 6.6), so a deficit of up to 12 points is not excluded; MyGene2 was public before its training data end. Split afterwards (unplanned), the agent solved more of the 54 distal arthrogryposes (14 : 6, p = 0.12) and GPT-6.1 more of the other 92 patients (8 : 20, p = 0.036) and of the 54 whose source paper was masked for the agent but not for GPT-6.1 (7 : 18, p = 0.043). v1.1 took a median of 17 seconds and US$0.008 per patient at list price, GPT-6.1 asked directly US$0.007 and its own model US$0.0005; v2 on the case reports US$0.010 per report.

## Discussion

In three prespecified tests on cases never used in their development, two revisions of the agent ranked the confirmed disease's identifier first more often than their own model and than their tools alone.

**Benchmark leakage can be large.** Masking each case's source paper halved LIRICAL's first-place accuracy on held-out published cases, and the project's own rankings lost as much, without changing their order — a fall of the size a recent preprint reported for the same LIRICAL release [13]. Unmasked benchmarks therefore favour them over models asked directly and over agents whose gains come from elsewhere. A paper-level mask is not an exact measure (see Knowledge and masking), but on the cases that stay answerable the drop is still from 62% to 35%.

**Tools and model are complementary.** On the patient sets the tools supplied the ranking, and on CADA's sparse records the model supplied more (14.7% against 11.6% for the tools alone).

**Retrieving the right entry is a contribution of tools, not a diagnosis.** A valid identifier for the wrong disease passes any format check; on the case reports, finding the right one is much of what the tools add. A clinician or a downstream tool needs that entry, but the diagnostic gain beyond it was not established by the name judge.

**External tests can catch what held-out tests miss.** The first version failed for a reason — ranking blind to prevalence — that phenotype matching has met before [3,30] and that its held-out test, with 42 cases of a relatively common disease among 840, did not reveal.

**Memorisation was avoided rather than measured.** Among the tests of the revised agent only the case reports postdate the model's reported cutoff, and there the lead held by identifier but not by name.

**Limitations.** The tests are retrospective, on public de-identified data, without clinicians, and use phenotype terms only; whether the evidence the agent records for each candidate helps clinicians was not tested. The agent used one model throughout; its training cutoff is known only from third-party reports, and MyGene2 and CADA were public before it. The mask could not act on CADA or on 92 of the 146 MyGene2 patients. Exact-match scoring asks for genetic subtypes a phenotype often cannot decide and favours systems that look identifiers up; the name judge was the systems' own model, unchecked by a clinician. Not every system ran on every set: v1.1 not on the case reports, v2 not on the patient sets, and the fixed pipeline, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Gemini 4 and, beyond MyGene2, GPT-6.1 not on the new sets. CADA's test file comes from a source that also supplied a development set. Each system was run once, and run-to-run variation at temperature 0 was not measured. On the case reports the answer key, the reader and the systems share one model, reports were kept only when the model's diagnosis resolved through the agent's name-matching algorithm, and about one report in ten stays readable.

## Conclusions

With each case's known source masked and every test fixed in advance, successive versions of a simple tool-using agent on an inexpensive model ranked the confirmed disease's identifier first more often than that model: v1.0 on held-out published cases, v1.1 on two patient sets and v2 on case reports published after the model's cutoff; on the last, much of the lead was retrieving the correct entry, and judged by name it was not significant. Its first version failed on external patients for a reason its held-out test did not reveal. Benchmarks should mask each case's source, test outside the development source, and fix the plan before the run.
