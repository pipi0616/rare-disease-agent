Language-model agents reportedly diagnose rare diseases from phenotypes with high accuracy, but these reports rest on published case reports that phenotype knowledge bases were curated from, that models may have read, and that differ from clinic patients. We built an agent on one inexpensive language model with tools over public phenotype knowledge, masked each case's known source paper from the tools, and fixed each test's plan before its run. Masking halved a published tool's first-place accuracy on 840 held-out cases (63.2% to 30.1%). The agent's first version led on these cases but lost to its own model on two external patient sets. In three prespecified tests on cases never used in development, two revisions of the agent ranked the confirmed disease's identifier first more often than their model asked directly: 52.1% versus 7.5% on 146 patients shared by families and clinicians (MyGene2), 23.6% versus 14.7% on 577 patients of diagnostic laboratories and clinics (CADA), and 19.6% versus 8.8% on 240 case reports published after the model's training cutoff (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001 each), at about one US cent per case. Judged by disease name, the lead held on MyGene2 and CADA but not on the case reports (21.7% versus 18.3%), where much of it was retrieval of the correct knowledge-base entry; it was not significant for CADA's 205 collaborators' patients, and GPT-6.1 asked directly did not differ significantly on MyGene2 (54.8%). Evaluations should mask case sources, test outside the development source, and fix the plan in advance.
