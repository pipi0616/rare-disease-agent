**Background:** Language-model agents are reported to diagnose rare diseases from a patient's phenotype with high accuracy, but these reports rest on published case reports that phenotype knowledge bases were curated from, that models may have read, and that differ from patients as clinics and laboratories record them.

**Objective:** This study aimed to evaluate a low-cost tool-using agent for phenotype-driven rare disease diagnosis under conditions that control these 3 problems and to measure how much benchmark accuracy depends on them.

**Methods:** We built an agent on 1 inexpensive large language model (deepseek-flash) with tools over public phenotype knowledge (Human Phenotype Ontology and Orphanet). Each case's known source paper was masked from everything the tools read, and each test followed a plan committed before its run. A first version (v1.0) was tested on 840 held-out published cases, 47 cases published after the model's training cutoff, and 2 external patient sets. Under 1 plan, v1.1 was then tested on 146 patients shared by families and clinicians (MyGene2) and 577 cases of diagnostic laboratories and clinics (the CADA test set), and v2, which adds Orphanet's diseases, on 240 case reports published after the cutoff. The primary outcome was ranking the confirmed diagnosis first, scored by disease identifier; for the revised agent it was compared with the same model asked directly. Answers were also judged by disease name.

**Results:** Masking halved the first-place accuracy of a published tool, LIRICAL, on the 840 held-out cases (63.2% to 30.1%). The first version led its own model and every other comparator on these cases but lost to its own model on both external patient sets. Revised, the agent ranked the confirmed disease's identifier first more often than its model in all 3 prespecified tests: 52.1% (95% CI 44.0%-60.0%) versus 7.5% on MyGene2, 23.6% (95% CI 20.3%-27.2%) versus 14.7% on CADA, and 19.6% (95% CI 15.1%-25.1%) versus 8.8% on the case reports (Holm-adjusted *P*<.001 for each), at about US $0.01 per case. Judged by name, the lead held on MyGene2 and CADA but not on the case reports (21.7% vs 18.3%; *P*=.18), where roughly half to two-thirds of it was retrieval of the correct knowledge-base entry. The lead was not significant for the 205 patients of CADA's collaborators' cohorts, and GPT-6.1 asked directly did not differ significantly from the agent on MyGene2 (54.8% vs 52.1%).

**Conclusions:** Two revisions of a simple tool-using agent improved on their own model, by identifier, in 3 prespecified tests on cases never used in their development. Masking case sources changed benchmark results substantially, and a held-out test missed a failure that external patients exposed. Evaluations should mask case sources, test outside the development source, and fix the analysis plan before the run.
